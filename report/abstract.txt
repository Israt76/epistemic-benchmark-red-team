Can an AI evaluation be improved without improving the AI? Epistemic benchmarks score how well a system knows what it knows, and those scores shape what developers optimise. We build the Epistemic Benchmark Integrity Test, a red-team procedure that attacks a scoring rule with answer-preserving manipulations: editing stated confidence, abstaining strategically, and directly optimising a confidence remap against the score. The attacker is tuned on one half of the data and judged on the other, against an explicit model of downstream decision quality. We audit ten scores in common use on 80 real classifiers and 9 real forecast archives, plus 150 controlled simulated systems. Expected calibration error is gameable by confidence edits alone: a single constant confidence removes a median 86% of the error for classifiers and 53% for forecasts (87% in simulation), while decisions get worse for 56% and 89% of those systems (71%). Strategic abstention games selective accuracy, hallucination rate, Brier score and log loss for at least 89% of systems in every setting. Proper scoring rules resist confidence-only attacks. A threshold-averaged net-benefit score that charges abstention its withheld value was not gamed under the primary yardstick. We also quantify metric shopping and question-mix sensitivity, and release the test as a reusable tool. No language model is evaluated; the results concern scoring rules. Epistemic evaluations should themselves be adversarially evaluated before they are trusted.
